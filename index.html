Evidence

Capacity

Two measures of whether processing keeps up. When fewer than 100 applications are completed for every 100 received, the backlog grows. Months of work is how long it would take to finish everything pending at that year's completion pace, if no new applications arrived. It holds that pace fixed, so it cannot show whether staffing, technology or policy will speed decisions up or slow them down: it measures the backlog against current output, not how long an applicant waits. USCIS publishes measured processing times separately.

Completed for every 100 received

Bars below the 100 line are years when the backlog grew.

Backlog in months of work at each year's pace

Pending applications divided by that year's monthly completions. Changes in capacity show up only after they change completions.

Changes in pending cases not explained by receipts and completions

In most years, pending at year end equals last year's pending plus receipts minus completions, within a few percent. Larger gaps usually mean cases were closed or moved without a decision, reclassified, or corrected in reporting. They are worth checking before drawing conclusions.

Modeled demand

Asylum demand: what global claims imply for USCIS

Applications received measure what people were able to file, not how many people seek protection. This section estimates demand for affirmative asylum (form I-589) from UNHCR's count of new asylum claims worldwide: world claims × US share of those claims × I-589 forms per 100 US claims. It covers asylum only; most other USCIS forms are limited by visa caps or eligibility rules, so global pressure shows up in waiting lists rather than filings. The estimate is speculative, and each assumption is a control you can change.

Method

Data notes

What this data can show

  • Workload and throughput at USCIS: how many applications arrive, how many are decided, and how many wait, by form and year.
  • Whether processing keeps pace, through completions per 100 received and months of work in the backlog.
  • Demand for citizenship and its pipeline, through naturalization, permanent residence and family petitions.

What it can't show

  • Every application counts the same. A green card renewal and an asylum case each count as one, though they take very different effort.
  • Causes. The data shows changes in receipts and completions, not why they changed (policy, staffing, fees, court rulings or demand).
  • The rest of the system. The USCIS data does not cover immigration courts (EOIR), visas issued abroad by the State Department, refugee admissions or border encounters. The Global context section adds UN and World Bank data on displacement and population, but not those US systems.
  • Demand that was never filed. Receipts count applications people were able to file. The Asylum demand section estimates asylum demand from UNHCR data; it is a modeled range, not a measurement.
  • People. One person may file several forms, so counts are applications, not individuals.
  • Completions changed definition. Through FY2019, completed means approved plus denied. From FY2020, USCIS reports total completions, which for some forms (asylum, credible and reasonable fear screenings, TPS) also include referrals to immigration court and administrative closures. Completions for those forms are not fully comparable before and after FY2020.

Compiling the quarterly reports (R)

  • USCIS changed the layout of its "All Forms" reports over time. Each layout is read with its own function: Excel reports from FY2024 on (rows 5–80), and three older CSV layouts (the 2014, 2018 and 2019–2020 formats), skipping their header rows.
  • Columns were renamed to the same names in every year: form, description, forms received, approved, denied, total completed and pending.
  • Thousands separators were removed, and dashes, which USCIS uses for "none", were set to 0.
  • In the older layouts, denials are spread over several columns. They were added together, and total completed was calculated as approved plus denied. Through FY2019, every completed count is approved plus denied; from FY2020 most forms use USCIS's reported total, which also counts cases closed in other ways. See "Completions changed definition" below.
  • Footnote numbers attached to form codes were removed (for example "I-58913" became I-589 and "N-40021" became N-400), and other variants were matched by pattern.
  • Each form was assigned to one of seven categories: citizenship, permanent residence, family-based, employment-based, humanitarian, other, and supplemental processing.
  • Rows without a description (section headings, totals and notes) were dropped.

Preparing the data for this dashboard (Python)

  • Read the file as Latin-1 and repaired garbled characters (for example "FiancÃ©e").
  • Merged codes for the same workload: N-600/600K into N-600, I-600/600A into I-600, I-800/800A into I-800, I-924/924A and I-924A into I-924, I-889 into I-899 (a typo; the two never overlap), and I-867 into I-870 (credible fear referral, replaced by the worksheet in FY2020).
  • FY2026 covers 9 months. Received and completed are annualized (multiplied by 12/9) where a full-year rate is needed; pending is never annualized.
  • Forms that never reached 1,000 receipts or pending cases are included in totals but not listed in the selector.
